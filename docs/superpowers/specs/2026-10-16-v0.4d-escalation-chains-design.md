# v0.4d — Escalation chains

Status: approved, not yet planned
Backlog: 4.9, and it settles 4.36
Milestone: v0.4d, the last open piece of the alerting line

## The problem

An alert route decides a fixed set of channels and delivers once. If
nobody reads that channel — the phone is on silent, the person is
asleep, the webhook is pointed at a dead endpoint — nothing further
happens. The alert stays open and unacknowledged, and Pulsix has said
everything it is ever going to say about it.

What is missing is persistence: after N minutes without an
acknowledgement, tell someone else. And its counterweight, which
matters just as much: stop the moment somebody is demonstrably dealing
with it, so a chain never wakes people who are not needed.

## The structural fact this design turns on

The existing follower is **event-driven**. It walks `alert_events` —
`opened`, `severity_raised`, `acknowledged` — and writes one row per
event into `alert_notify_state`, whose primary key *is*
`alert_event_id`. One row per event, for the whole organization.

Escalation is **time-driven**. It fires because nothing happened. Its
state is per `(alert, route, step)`, which has no home in a table keyed
by event id. So escalation is a second axis beside the existing one,
not a variation of it, and it gets its own table and its own sweep.

## Decisions

### 1. The chain hangs off the route

Each route carries an ordered list of steps. Step 0 is implicit: the
route's existing channels or rotation, fired at once, exactly as today
— so no existing route changes behaviour or needs migrating. Steps 1
and up are new.

Routes already add up rather than override (v0.4b, decision from
backlog 4.1b). An alert matched by two routes therefore runs two
chains, independently. That is coherent under the reading a route
already has: *who hears about this, and how persistently*.

Rejected: a separate reusable escalation-policy object that routes
reference. It is the better answer once several routes share one
ladder, and v0.4c's rotations are the precedent for that shape — but
building it now adds a third object to understand before anything
escalates at all. If repetition becomes painful, extracting it is a
contained change, because the steps already live behind a route.

### 2. A voided acknowledgement stays voided

`alerts.acknowledged_severity` is cleared when the `ack_voided` event is
written.

Today "acknowledged" is a live comparison, `severity <=
acknowledged_severity`, and that column is never cleared. So an alert
acked at `warn`, risen to `crit` (which writes `ack_voided` and makes
the row read unacknowledged), then fallen back to `warn`, reads as
acknowledged again — while its own timeline shows the void. The row and
the history disagree. That is backlog 4.36, filed during v0.4a and
deliberately left for whichever milestone first depended on it.

Escalation is that milestone: the disagreement stops being cosmetic and
becomes the difference between a chain that continues and one that does
not.

Clearing the column is chosen over teaching escalation to read the
events, because two notions of "acknowledged" living side by side is
exactly the defect, not a workaround for it. The substantive argument:
the alert got worse since a human last looked at it, so asking that
human to confirm again is the point rather than an imposition.

Accepted cost: an alert flapping across a severity boundary asks for an
acknowledgement more than once.

### 3. The clock measures time in which someone could have reacted

Two rules, and together they are the whole timing model.

**The base is observed, not computed.** When the follower delivers for
a `(alert, route)` pair for the first time, it writes a step-0 row with
`fired_at = now`. That moment is the base. The alternative — deriving
"the first moment this route could deliver" from the window's cron
expression — would mean searching forward for the next opening, which
is fiddly and hostile across a DST boundary. Observing it is correct by
construction for every case at once: a breakthrough severity, a shut
window, a suppression that lifts later.

**Delays are relative to the previous step**, not absolute from the
alert. Step *k* becomes due `delay_k` after step *k−1* actually fired.
This is how people describe escalation ("if nobody answers in five
minutes, then…"), and it makes a pile-up structurally impossible: a
step's clock cannot start before its predecessor has really gone out,
so a chain can never arrive all at once after a long silence.

**The window interaction needs no new setting.** `windowOpen` already
returns true for a severity in the route's breakthrough set
(`follow_resolve.go:275`), so a breakthrough `crit` is delivered at
02:00 through a shut window — which makes its base 02:00 and its steps
fall on time. A non-breakthrough severity waits for the window, so its
base is the opening and the ladder gets its minutes of real attention.
One configurable behaviour, per severity, through a control that
already exists and already means "this severity ignores the window".

This does widen the breakthrough set's meaning: today it governs
delivery, and it will now also govern when the clock starts. That is
the same sentence applied twice, but it is a widening and is recorded
as one.

### 4. What stops the chain, and what only pauses it

| condition | effect |
|---|---|
| acknowledged (under decision 2) | stop |
| closed, `resolved` or `stale` | stop |
| severity leaves the route's severity set | **pause** — the route stops matching, and matches again if it returns |
| route disabled | **pause** |
| route deleted | stop |
| muted: snooze, operator silence, maintenance window | **pause** |

Only the first two rows are final. Everything else is a pause, and
decision 5 says what resuming costs: the next step gets its full delay
again, measured from the moment the alert became urgent once more.

`alerts.Mute` is already the single place all three mute reasons are
compared (`internal/alerts/mute.go`), and the follower already consults
it. A muted alert is one nobody is being told about, so no step fires
while it holds.

Snooze pauses rather than stops. A snooze carries an "until"; someone
who snoozes for an hour does not expect the ladder to have been
silently abandoned when the hour is up. And because delays are relative
(decision 3), resuming cannot produce a burst.

### 5. Resuming gives the next step its full delay again

The spec's first draft said a chain "stops" when the severity leaves the
route's set and "continues" after a voided acknowledgement, without
saying from where. Both are resumptions, and both need the same rule.

A step is due when `now >= eligible_since + delay_seconds`, where
`eligible_since` is the **latest** of:

- the previous step's `fired_at` (step 0's, for step 1);
- the alert's most recent `severity_raised` or `ack_voided` event;
- the end of the most recent mute — the `unsnoozed` event when a human
  cleared it, the alert's own `snoozed_until` when the snooze simply ran
  out, or the end of the maintenance occurrence that covered it.

Nothing new is stored. Every one of those is already on the alert, its
events, or the occurrences the pass computes anyway.

The rule reads as one sentence: **a step gets its full delay after the
last thing that made this alert newly urgent.** So an alert that drops
out of a route's severity set and comes back does not have its skipped
steps fire at once; the ladder picks up where it was and gives the next
rung its minutes. The same holds after a voided acknowledgement and
after any mute lifts.

Steps already fired are never re-fired. `alert_escalations` rows are
written once and never deleted, so the chain only moves forward, no
matter how often an alert leaves and re-enters.

Worth naming because it is easy to get wrong: a snooze that **expires**
writes no event at all. `Unsnooze` is a human action and writes
`unsnoozed` (`internal/alerts/store_sql.go:205`), but a bounded snooze
reaching its `snoozed_until` just stops being reported by `alerts.Mute`.
That is why the anchor list names the column and not only the event.

### 6. A step is reached once, and names its own channels

A step notifies its own channels when it fires, once. Earlier steps are
not re-notified — they already heard at their own moment. Repeats
("tell channel 1 again every ten minutes") are a different feature and
are out of scope.

A step takes either a fixed channel list or a rotation, the same two
sources the route itself offers, resolved through the existing
`channelsOf` (`follow_resolve.go:217`). That makes "step 2 is whoever is
on call in the escalation rotation" expressible without new machinery.

### 7. An escalation is visible on the alert, not only in the channel

A new `alert_events` kind, `escalated`, carrying the step number and the
channels reached in its `payload`. Without it the alert page shows an
untouched timeline while phones are ringing.

This replaces the `kind` CHECK constraint
(`00049_alerts.sql:138`), which today admits seven values. The effect is
additive — no existing row becomes invalid — but it is formally a DROP
and ADD of the constraint, and the migration says so.

## Data

```sql
-- the ladder, per route
CREATE TABLE alert_route_steps (
  route_id      uuid NOT NULL,
  position      int  NOT NULL CHECK (position >= 1),  -- 0 is the route itself
  delay_seconds int  NOT NULL CHECK (delay_seconds > 0),
  rotation_id   uuid,            -- or NULL, when the step names channels
  PRIMARY KEY (route_id, position)
);

-- which steps have fired, and when
CREATE TABLE alert_escalations (
  alert_id  uuid        NOT NULL,
  route_id  uuid        NOT NULL,
  position  int         NOT NULL,   -- 0 is the base: the first real delivery
  fired_at  timestamptz NOT NULL,
  PRIMARY KEY (alert_id, route_id, position)
);
```

Both carry `org_id` with the composite foreign keys and the row-level
security policy pair every tenant table in this schema carries; the
plan spells those out. A step's fixed channels live in a join table
beside `alert_route_steps`, mirroring `alert_route_channels`.

`alert_escalations` is an anti-join ledger, the same shape v0.4b's
delivery ledger uses: a due step is one with no row, and writing the row
is what makes it fired. No cursor, no "next due at" column to drift.

## The sweep

The follower's `Tick` gains a second pass, inside the same transaction
and the same advisory lock as the first. The existing event pass is
untouched.

The second pass asks: which open, unacknowledged, unmuted alerts have a
matching enabled route whose next unfired step is due? Due means
`now >= previous step's fired_at + delay_seconds`. For each, it resolves
the step's channels, enqueues through the existing outbox, writes the
`alert_escalations` row and the `escalated` event.

Reading before writing matters here for the same reason it did in
v0.4b: a step fired in this pass must not become due again within the
same tick against the identical `now`.

## Risks

- **A chain that will not stop is worse than no chain.** Every stopping
  condition in decision 4 needs a test that proves the chain is silent
  after it, not merely that the condition was recorded.
- **Clearing `acknowledged_severity` changes v0.4a behaviour** for code
  that never asked about escalation. The alert list's acknowledged
  filter, the host card and the timeline all read that column. Each needs
  checking against the new semantics, not just the follower.
- **Two routes, two chains** is correct by decision 1 but surprising the
  first time someone meets it. The host's route card has to make the
  overlap visible, or an operator will read one ladder and be woken by
  the other.
- **Time in tests.** The follower's clock is already injected; every
  timing test drives it rather than sleeping.

## Acceptance

- A crit that nobody acknowledges reaches step 1 after its delay, step 2
  after its own, each one once, each one visible on the alert's timeline.
- Acknowledging between two steps stops the chain; the later step never
  fires. The same for closing, for the severity leaving the route's set,
  and for disabling the route.
- An alert acked at `warn`, risen to `crit`, fallen back to `warn` reads
  as unacknowledged everywhere — list, host card, timeline — and its
  chain continues.
- A muted alert fires no step while muted and resumes afterwards without
  a burst — proven for all three mute reasons, including a snooze that
  expires on its own rather than being cleared by a person.
- An alert that leaves a route's severity set and re-enters it resumes at
  the next unfired step, and that step gets its full delay from the
  moment of re-entry rather than firing at once.
- A shut window delays the base; a breakthrough severity does not.
- An end-to-end run drives a real agent's alert through two steps with
  the follower's own clock advanced, and asserts the second channel was
  reached only after the first had been given its time.
- `make test`, `golangci-lint run`, the SPA suite, the coverage gates and
  `npm run e2e` all pass.
